# Voice

## The test

**Would you say this out loud to someone who's had a hard year?**

That's the whole rule. Most of our readers are eleven months into a job search and have started to
suspect the problem is them. Marketing language that would be merely bland to a confident reader
lands as patronising to a worn-out one.

## Five characteristics

### 1. Specific, to the point of discomfort
"Applying for months" is wallpaper. "Two hundred applications. Four replies." is a sentence someone
recognises themselves in. Use numbers, names and concrete nouns.

We're the product that says specifics beat summaries. The copy has to behave the way the product
does.

### 2. On their side, never above them
We're not experts fixing a broken candidate. The candidate isn't broken; the format is.

> **No:** "Optimise your profile to stand out to recruiters."
> **Yes:** "Your job title doesn't do you justice."

### 3. Plain, and slightly understated
No exclamation marks. No "revolutionary", "game-changing", "supercharge". Where everyone else
shouts, a flat declarative sentence reads as confidence.

> **No:** "Transform your career with AI-powered matching!"
> **Yes:** "Companies come to you because of what you've built."

### 4. Honest about limits
We say what we don't do, what isn't finished, and what we can't promise. It's the only way the
privacy claims get believed.

> "We don't promise you a job. We promise that what a company sees is backed by evidence."

### 5. Warm where it costs us something
Warmth in a headline is cheap. Warmth in an error message, an empty state, or a rejection is where
it's real.

> "You never owe anyone a reason." — the decline button
> "Nothing you've written is lost — it's still here when you try again." — a failed save

## Words we don't use

| Avoid | Use | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Talent, candidates *(to their face)* | you, people | Nobody describes themselves as talent. |
| Opportunities | who's interested | Name what it feels like to receive. |
| Apply | reach out, start a conversation | We don't have applications. Never imply we do. |
| Leverage, utilise, unlock, empower | use, do, build | Consultant language. |
| Journey, solution, seamless | — | Means nothing. |
| Rockstar, ninja, A-player | — | The vocabulary of the thing we're replacing. |
| 10x, game-changing, revolutionary | — | We're understated. |
| Hustle, grind | — | Cruel to someone already exhausted. |

## Rewrites

> **Before:** "Leverage our AI-powered platform to unlock opportunities tailored to your skillset."
> **After:** "Describe one thing you built. Companies search for what it proves."

> **Before:** "Join thousands of top talent on the fastest-growing hiring platform."
> **After:** "Free, always. Hidden until you say otherwise. Start with one project — about ten minutes."

> **Before:** "Complete your profile to maximise visibility!"
> **After:** "One project reads as a one-off. Two start to look like a pattern — which is what an employer is trying to see."

## Writing for companies

Same voice, different pressure. Companies get the respect of being told the harder thing.

> "You're not writing a job description. Describe the actual work, because that's what we match
> evidence against — and it's what a good candidate reads to decide whether you're serious."

Never flatter them about their "mission" or "amazing culture". They can tell.

## Punctuation and formatting

- Sentence case everywhere. Never Title Case For Headings.
- Full stops in headlines. *"Your job title doesn't do you justice."*
- British English: organise, realise, behaviour, whilst → while.
- £ before numbers, commas in thousands: £65,000.
- Em dashes for asides — like this — not parentheses.
- No emoji in product copy. Sparingly, if ever, in social.
